# Question themes for SAT Reading and Writing (reference notes)

Notes taken on 2026-10-25 from the verbal half of *Barron's Digital SAT Practice Questions 2024* (about 310 Reading and Writing questions in 12 sections). They record **what kinds** of texts, topics and question types the book uses, so nurzycom can write **its own** questions on the same ground. Barron's text is copyrighted: never copy or lightly reword its passages, questions or answer choices. Use these notes for topics, genres and question shapes only, with new passages built on well-established facts.

## How the book's verbal part is built

| Section | Questions | Typical stem |
|---|---|---|
| Words in Context | 25 | "As used in the text, what does the word '…' most nearly mean?" (14 of 25), plus blank-fill "most logical and precise word or phrase" |
| Structure and Purpose | 25 | function of the underlined sentence / phrase / quotation; overall structure; main purpose |
| Cross-Text Connections | 25 | how the author of Text 2 would respond; what both texts have in common; how a term is used differently |
| Central Ideas and Details | 25 | main idea; "according to the text"; "which is **not** true" detail questions |
| Quantitative Evidence | 25 | "uses data from the graph / table / chart to complete the statement / example" |
| Textual Evidence | 25 | "finding, if true, would most directly support / **undermine** the claim"; "which quotation most effectively illustrates the claim" (poems, plays, speeches) |
| Inferences | 25 | "most logically completes the text" |
| Number and Tense Agreement | 27 | subject–verb agreement, verb tense and form, participles |
| Punctuation | 27 | commas with supplements, semicolons, colons, dashes, possessive apostrophes |
| Sentence Structure and Organization | 27 | fragments, run-ons, dangling modifiers, parallelism, comparisons, word forms (comparing / comparison / comparable) |
| Transitions | 27 | logical transition |
| Notes Analysis (Rhetorical Synthesis) | 27 | student notes + a goal: emphasize, summarize, present a cause, present a nonbiased view, emphasize irony |

Compared with the official Bluebook style, the book leans much more on **excerpts from published works** (literature, essays, speeches, government reports), often several questions from the same source.

## Source genres and themes

**Literature (fiction, drama, poetry), mostly pre-1930.** American short stories of the 1920s (flappers, manners, irony); turn-of-the-century novels of conscience at sea; 19th-century American women writers on work and independence; Victorian comedy of manners (Wilde) set against American realism (Alcott); Romantic and Victorian poetry (Blake, Tennyson, Freneau's storm poem, Drayton's farewell to a lover); Restoration verse criticism (Dryden); 18th-century essays on fiction (Samuel Johnson); 18th-century letters on women's education (Edgeworth); 20th-century Asian and Asian American fiction (re-education in China; Japanese American identity after World War II internment).
*nurzycom use:* public-domain excerpts quoted exactly (only texts we know word for word), or original prose described as original. Never invent quotations attributed to real authors.

**Essays, speeches, history.** Tocqueville on American democracy (associations, equality, aristocracy); Manifest Destiny (O'Sullivan, 1839); Washington's character (Van Dyke); Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk* (double consciousness, the color line); Native American life and assimilation (Ohiyesa/Charles Eastman; Matthiessen); women's rights speeches of the 1850s–1870s (Abby Kelley Foster, Frances Willard; arguments on "woman's subjection"); FDR's "second Bill of Rights"; the Boer War; Hiroshima and the decision to drop the bomb (a veteran's view versus a historian's hindsight); the "clash of civilizations" debate; neoconservative views of commercial society; the art and philosophy of teaching; the professionalization of poetry and what poets can learn from songwriters; reading and book-collecting habits; the sophists; Fermat's Last Theorem.

**Science and health (often government or agency texts).** Wildlife disease and why it matters to people; sustainability and renewable resources; nuclear energy and sustainable development; tidal friction and the habitability of exoplanets; Hawaiian hotspots and mantle plumes; coastal erosion and sea level rise; Titan and the solar wind (Cassini); the adolescent brain and culture; memory training; early modern medicine (delirium tremens, the arrival of effective drugs in the 1930s); acupuncture; allergies; phrenology (Gall); atmospheric water generators; rabbit hemorrhagic disease; oak wilt fungus; soil biodiversity; vegetarian diets; dancing and the brain; pets and health.

**Data topics (graphs and tables).** Women's and men's earnings and occupations; adult literacy by region; mercury in fish in national parks; toxoplasmosis; doctorate degrees by field; growth of healthcare employment; malaria cases by month of travel; out-of-school children; births by month (marketing); school enrollment projections by state; labor force change; dairy production; deaths by cause, age and state; age of first-time mothers by country; onion yields; traffic deaths; dependency ratio; labor productivity; land the US bought between 1781 and 1867.

**Writing-section topics (grammar items set in content).** Roman banquets; the origins of women's subordination (19th-century argument); mercury in national-park fish; careers in ornithology; civilization and social order; the "canon" of proportions in Egyptian art; stained-glass technique; an acupuncturist's practice; healthcare jobs; early maps of Cuba and Columbus in Hispaniola; typhoons and sea level rise in Indonesia (Java, Sulawesi); FDR's economic rights; a visit to a Japanese music club; the Arctic as a natural laboratory and aurora; Ohiyesa's schooling; wild mushrooms (edible and poisonous *Amanita*); retiring to Texas; a poodle as watchdog; stuffed dates.

**Notes topics (rhetorical synthesis).** Shared symbols across cultures; abbreviations; the Yeti (nonbiased view); acerola as a cash crop; the Pilgrims' escape from persecution; acids; hospital acoustics; pilot skill; the Alaska boundary dispute; Louisa May Alcott and *Little Women*; GMO and organic crops; algae as food; why English is hard to learn; aluminum; amaranth; early American literature before 1850; what makes an antique; armadillos expanding their range; Spanish vaqueros and the cattle industry; Jumbo the elephant; declining bees; beef and health research; the history of bells; the 2020 jump in household saving; the irony of plant-based butter.

## Patterns worth copying (as patterns, not text)

- **Several questions on one source.** A set of 3–5 questions from one excerpt (vocabulary, purpose, inference) trains students to reread efficiently.
- **"Most nearly mean" with common words used in an uncommon sense** ("rise" = reaction, "tidy" = orderly, "illumination" = intellectual enlightenment). The trap is the everyday meaning.
- **"Not true" detail questions:** three choices are stated in the text, one is not.
- **Undermine/weaken questions** alongside support questions.
- **Quotation questions on poems:** a claim about the speaker's attitude, four quoted lines, one of which fits.
- **Paired literary texts** on the same theme from different eras (comedy of manners versus realist novel; two poems by one author).
- **Grammar items inside real-sounding informational prose** (statistics reports, career guides) rather than isolated sentences.
- **Rhetorical-synthesis goals beyond "emphasize":** summarize, present a likely cause, present a nonbiased perspective, emphasize irony, suggest a rationale.
